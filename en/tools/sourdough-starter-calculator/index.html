Feeding: how much starter, flour and water

Choose how much starter you need and the feeding ratio; the calculator splits the weight. For 200 g at 1:1:1 that is 67 g starter, 67 g flour and 67 g water. If tomorrow's dough needs 150 g of levain, build a little more than that (200 g) so some is left to keep the culture going.

The ratio sets the timing. With wheat flour in a 75-79 °F (24-26 °C) kitchen, a healthy starter typically doubles in roughly 4 to 6 hours at 1:1:1 and needs considerably longer at 1:5:5 or 1:10:10, the ratios people use for an overnight build. Treat those times as a rough guide: every culture, flour and kitchen runs at its own pace, and the reliable sign is volume doubled with bubbles all through.

Converting a yeasted recipe to sourdough

Under «Swap yeast», enter the recipe's flour and water and the starter percentage you want (on the flour). For 500 g flour and 350 g water with 20 % starter at 100 % hydration you use 100 g of starter and add only 450 g flour and 300 g water, because the starter already brings 50 g of each. Hydration stays at 70 % and the salt stays the same (about 10 g, 2 %). All the commercial yeast goes.

What does change is time: a naturally leavened dough ferments noticeably slower than a yeasted one and usually benefits from a long cold retard in the fridge. Watch the dough, not the clock.

Fresh, active dry and instant yeast

All three are the same baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) with different moisture. For the same rise: active dry = fresh × 0.4 and instant = fresh ÷ 3. 25 g of fresh yeast is 10 g of active dry or 8.3 g of instant. Active dry is traditionally proofed in warm water first; instant (rapid-rise, bread machine yeast) goes straight into the flour. There is no fixed yeast-to-starter equivalent, because a starter's strength depends on how active it is; that is why it is measured as a percentage of the flour.

Frequently asked questions

What does a 1:1:1 or 1:5:5 feeding mean?

Parts by weight of starter, flour and water. 1:1:1 is, say, 50 g starter, 50 g flour and 50 g water. 1:5:5 is 20 g starter with 100 g flour and 100 g water: the culture is more diluted, so it takes longer to peak, which suits an overnight feed.

How much starter replaces yeast in a recipe?

Most bakers use 15 to 25 % active starter on the flour weight. More starter ferments faster and tastes tangier; less gives a longer, milder rise. The calculator takes the flour and water inside the starter out of the recipe so the dough feels the same.

How do I convert instant yeast to active dry or fresh?

By leavening power, instant ≈ fresh ÷ 3 and active dry ≈ fresh × 0.4. One US packet (¼ oz, 7 g) of instant yeast is about 21 g of fresh yeast or 8.4 g of active dry.

Why isn't my starter rising after feeding?

Usually it is too cold (below 68 °F / 20 °C everything slows right down), the feed is too diluted for the time you give it, or the starter is still young. Try 1:1:1 somewhere warm and use it once it has doubled.

What is starter hydration?

Water divided by flour, times 100. A 100 % starter has equal weights of water and flour; a stiff starter at 50-60 % has about half as much water. Change it under «More options» if yours is not 100 %.
